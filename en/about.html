Understanding the business before analysing the data

Victor Ramos Alves de Matos — Data & Business Analytics. Juiz de Fora, Minas Gerais, Brazil.

I started my career understanding companies from the inside, through accounting and corporate finance. That meant eight years of month-end close, reconciliation, statutory filings and financial reporting — including the environment of a publicly listed company, with enterprise ERP, formal controls and standardised processes.

That experience gave me something rarely learned afterwards: the operational reality behind business data. I know where a number comes from, which system produced it, at what point in the process it usually breaks, and what it means to the person deciding based on it.

Today I combine that domain knowledge with computer science, data analytics, programming and data engineering. Not as a career switch, but as a continuation: the same question as always — is this number right, and what does it mean? — answered with better tools.

Why data

The turning point happened at work, not in a classroom. I began automating the repetitive steps of the accounting close in Python, and it became obvious how much professional judgement was being spent on tasks that only required patience. The next step was natural: if extraction can be automated, it can be modelled; if it can be modelled, it can actually be analysed.

Chicago Marathon Analytics came out of that logic. I took a domain I care about personally — running — and applied the full cycle: ingestion, identity resolution, dimensional modelling, SQL analysis and visualisation. The data-quality problems I found there are the same ones from an accounting reconciliation: the same entity described differently by different systems. The scale changes, the nature does not.

How I work

  • Model before analysing. A well-modelled base makes analysis trivial and, more importantly, makes results agree with each other.
  • Document the process. A routine that only exists in the head of whoever runs it is an operational risk, not an asset.
  • Distrust the number that looks right. It is the reflex that reconciliation trains, and it applies equally to a balance sheet and a pipeline.
  • Ship it reproducible. If you cannot re-run it from scratch and get the same result, it is not finished.
