Why publish

Cleaning is the work; publishing is the proof

The expensive part of any analysis happens before the first chart: understanding the source format, reconciling names that change between seasons, deciding what to do with the missing field. That work is normally invisible — it disappears inside a notebook nobody opens.

Publishing each cleaned base makes the effort verifiable. Whoever opens the file sees the structure, the column dictionary and the limitations I recorded; whoever disagrees with one of my decisions can redo the analysis themselves. It is the same principle as the case studies on this site, applied to the raw material.

The football sets are also the raw material behind Football Intel: whatever the dashboard consumes, anyone can download and check.

Method

What every published base carries

1

A row means exactly one thing

The grain is declared and held: one match, one athlete in one race, one player in one match, one card. Without that, any sum a user computes counts the same event twice.

2

Seasons standardised before being merged

Source files arrive with column names, date formats and team spellings that shift from one year to the next. Reconciliation happens before the join — that is what lets you compare 2020 with 2026 without a footnote.

3

Absence declared, not filled in

When a year is missing, the description says why — Chicago 2020 was cancelled, and that is written down. An empty field stays empty; none of my bases estimate a number to plug a hole.

4

Identifiable people get different treatment

In the Juiz de Fora ranking, the 17,787 runners are amateurs from one city, not public athletes. Names were pseudonymised before publication: performance analysis remains possible, individual identification does not.
